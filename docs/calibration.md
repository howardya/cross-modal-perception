# Calibration — What Is Measured and What Is Modelled

**Read this first.**

> This project **models** expert attention. It does not **measure** it.
>
> No eye-tracking data was collected. No expert's gaze was recorded. Every
> persona difference shown in the demo is generated by a language model
> instructed with a role mandate, constrained by effect sizes taken from
> published research on *other* domains, and checked against those constraints
> automatically. That is a defensible way to build an illustration. It is not
> evidence about how credit analysts read.

Written for someone who wants to disbelieve the demo. Section 6 is the list of
reasons they would be right to.

---

## 1. What the demo actually claims

| The demo shows | The honest claim behind it |
|---|---|
| Four professionals attending to different clauses | Four *mandates*, applied to the same text by a language model |
| A clause turning green for one role and red for another | A modelled valence judgment; the underlying conflict of interest is real, the specific score is not measured |
| "They overlap on 31% of this note" | A Jensen-Shannon divergence between two modelled distributions, computed exactly |
| "Experts suppress the irrelevant twice as hard as they enhance the relevant" | A real meta-analytic finding, from medicine/sport/aviation, *assumed* to transfer to financial prose |
| The bouba/kiki result | Measured, on 917 people across 25 languages. Real. |
| The synaesthesia illustration | An illustration of *reported experience*. The measured advantage is much smaller — see §5 |

The two Act 1 items are the only things in the piece grounded in direct
measurement of the phenomenon being displayed. Everything in Act 2 is modelled.

## 2. The three calibration layers

### L1 — Literature as executable constraints

Implemented in `pipeline/src/cmp/targets.py`, tested in `tests/test_targets.py`.

Published effect sizes are converted into constraints that generated fields must
satisfy. A field pair passes only if all of the following hold:

| Constraint | Source |
|---|---|
| Expert attends *more* to task-relevant units than novice | Gegenfurtner et al. 2011 (r = 0.27) |
| Expert attends *less* to task-irrelevant units than novice | Gegenfurtner et al. 2011 (r = −0.43) |
| Suppression of the irrelevant *dominates* enhancement of the relevant | The asymmetry between those two |
| Expert salience is more concentrated than novice salience | Information-reduction hypothesis |

**What is deliberately not gated.** The exact magnitude. The literature target is
an asymmetry ratio of ≈ 1.70, and the code reports the distance from it, but does
not fail a field for missing it. Two of the three numbers behind 1.70 are
unverified (§6.3); gating on it would claim precision the evidence does not have.

**A modelling subtlety worth stating.** Salience is normalised to a fixed budget,
because Sirois et al. measured attention as zero-sum within a document. Under a
fixed budget with equal numbers of relevant and irrelevant units, enhancement and
suppression sum to 2 — each is then merely a restatement of the other, and the
asymmetry constraint is vacuous. It only carries information when irrelevant
units outnumber relevant ones. That is also the realistic shape of a document,
so the constraint does real work on real stimuli, but it would silently do
nothing on a toy one.

### L2 — Persona scoring

Implemented in `pipeline/src/cmp/scoring.py` and `anthropic_client.py`.

- Each persona is defined as a **mandate**: objective, time horizon, loss
  function, and what it reads for. No temperament, no personality. This follows
  Haider & Frensch's finding that information reduction is instruction-sensitive
  — a reader's filter is a function of the task they believe they are doing.
- Each persona scores every clause on salience, valence, chunk and arousal, under
  a constrained output schema.
- **Every persona is sampled k times** (default 5) and the runs are aggregated by
  **median**, so a single wild sample cannot move a unit's score. Chunk labels
  aggregate by mode, since they are labels rather than magnitudes.
- The prompt explicitly enforces the finite-budget and dilution constraints. Without
  them, personas mark almost everything as important and the fields stop being
  distinguishable — the model's default is to be comprehensive, which is exactly
  the opposite of what expertise looks like.

### L3 — Reliability and validation

- **Inter-run agreement** is computed as Krippendorff's alpha (interval) across
  the k samples, per persona, and reported in the demo. Convention: ≥ 0.80 supports
  firm conclusions, 0.667–0.80 tentative ones, below that nothing.
- **Held-out validation**: parameters are fitted on the hero stimulus and checked
  on a second stimulus not used for fitting. Whether it holds is reported either
  way.

**Reliability bounds noise, not bias.** A high alpha means the model says the same
thing every time. It does not mean the model is right. A persona could be
consistently, reproducibly wrong about how credit analysts read, and alpha would
not notice.

## 3. Face-validity check (built, not yet run)

`pipeline/src/cmp/human.py` compares a real person's markup of the hero note to
each persona's salience vector, and ranks them.

The protocol: give a practitioner the unstyled note, ask them to highlight what
matters to them, record their role, and compare their markup to the persona
claiming to represent them.

**Status: not run.** No human data has been collected. Even n = 3 would move this
from "plausible model" to "checked against humans", and a *mismatch* would be a
stronger result than a match — it would be the first real evidence in the project.

If this remains unrun at demo time, the demo must say so.

## 4. What the numbers in the demo mean

| Number | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Perceptual overlap | 1 − Jensen-Shannon divergence (base 2) between normalised salience | 1.0 identical attention, 0.0 completely disjoint |
| Valence conflicts | Count of clauses where two personas' valence signs oppose and both exceed 0.5 | Clauses read in genuinely opposite directions |
| Chunk agreement | Adjusted Rand index between chunk partitions | Chance-corrected; ~0 means unrelated groupings |
| Concentration | 1 − normalised Shannon entropy of salience | 0 attention spread evenly, 1 all on one clause |
| Reliability (alpha) | Krippendorff's alpha, interval, across k samples | Agreement of a persona with itself |

All are standard statistics, computed exactly, unit-tested against hand-computed
values. **The arithmetic is not where the uncertainty lives — the inputs are.**

## 5. Two corrections the research forced

The original plan asserted two things that turned out to be wrong. Both are
corrected in the build, and stating them is part of the demo.

**Bouba/kiki is 72%, not ~95%.** Ćwiek et al. (2022), 917 participants, 25
languages, 10 writing systems: 72% congruent [56–82%]. The effect is asymmetric —
*bouba* is reliable in 22 of 25 languages, *kiki* in only 11 — and it goes *below
chance* in Romanian (36%), Mandarin and Turkish. Roughly one viewer in four will
not match, and the demo treats that as a data point rather than an error.

**Synaesthetic "pop-out" is not pop-out.** Ward et al. (2010), 36 synaesthetes:
41.4% correct against 31.5% for controls. The authors concluded synaesthetic
colour does *not* guide attention the way real colour does, and Rothen & Meier
(2009) failed to replicate the advantage at all. The advantage is real but partial.

The corrected framing is better for the argument. A synaesthete has a head start,
not magic — and so does an expert. Both are partial, both are fallible, and both
make some things effortless at the cost of making others invisible.

## 6. Reasons to disbelieve this

In descending order of seriousness.

1. **No study measures the roles being contrasted.** No published eye-tracking
   compares a credit analyst, an equity PM, a risk officer and a retail investor
   on the same document. The role differences are argued from mandates. This is
   the single largest weakness and no amount of careful arithmetic downstream
   repairs it.

2. **The best finance-domain anchor did not use professionals.** Sirois et al.
   (2018) used 98 post-graduate accounting students as proxies for
   non-professional investors.

3. **Two calibration constants are unverified.** r = 0.27 and r = −0.43 come from
   secondary sources; the primary article is paywalled. Flagged ⚠️ throughout, and
   in `pipeline/src/cmp/targets.py`.

4. **Cross-domain transfer is assumed.** The effect sizes come from medicine,
   sport, aviation and music. Applying them to financial prose is an assumption no
   study licenses.

5. **The expertise literature contradicts itself.** Klostermann & Moeinirad (2020),
   101 studies, found fixation duration and count unreliable as expertise markers,
   with more null than significant results — and the sport literature's direction
   is the *opposite* of the visualization meta-analysis's. The model responds by
   building on gaze *location*, which survives both, but this is a live dispute.

6. **A language model is not an expert.** Persona output is a model's model of a
   role, shaped by how that role is written about in its training data. Sampling
   and aggregation bound the variance; nothing here bounds the bias.

7. **The stimulus is constructed.** The hero note is written to contain clauses
   that invert across roles. That makes the effect legible, and also makes it
   larger than it would be on an arbitrary document.

8. **No human has validated any of it** (§3).

---

## 7. What would fix it

Roughly in order of value per unit of effort:

1. Run the face-validity check with 3–5 practitioners per role. One afternoon.
2. Obtain the paywalled meta-analysis and verify the two constants.
3. Run the held-out validation on a second stimulus and publish the result.
4. Compare against a real analyst note rather than only a constructed one.
5. Actual eye-tracking of 5 practitioners per role. This is the real study, and it
   appears not to exist. If someone wants a research project, that is it.
