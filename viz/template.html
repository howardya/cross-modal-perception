Cross-modal perception · perceptual expertise

Someone else's eyes

You cannot see your own way of seeing. It is the thing doing the looking. This page lends you four other ways, one at a time, over a single page of text.

One sentence from the note below “The board approved a $300m share repurchase programme, to be funded from the revolving credit facility.” To someone without training Good news. They're giving money back to shareholders. To every professional who read it Bad news. Borrowing, at 4.1x leverage, to buy its own stock.

Same words. The untrained reader is not being careless — that sentence is written to sound like generosity. Knowing where the money comes from is what flips it, and that knowledge is invisible from the outside.

This page has four professionals read the whole document. What separates them is not what they think of it. It is what they can see. Scroll.

Now the same idea, learned.

A grapheme–colour synaesthete has a head start on that grid because the digits arrive already sorted. A credit analyst has a head start on a financial document for the same structural reason — years of practice have made some sentences loud and the rest quiet.

The mechanism is not sharper sight. It is learned neglect. The strongest finding in the expertise literature is not that experts look harder at what matters; it is that they stop seeing what doesn't. That is what the rest of this page shows, and it is why parts of the text below will be hard to read.

Nothing is ever actually hidden from you. Hover or tab to any clause and it returns to full clarity. You can always choose to look. The expert doesn't have to choose — that is the whole difference.

How much of this to believe

What is measured, and what is modelled

Most of this page is a model, not a measurement. Here is the line, drawn where it actually falls rather than where it would flatter the work.

  • measured The bouba/kiki result: 72% congruent across 917 people, 25 languages and 10 writing systems (Ćwiek et al. 2022). Not the ~95% usually quoted, and it falls below chance in Romanian, Mandarin and Turkish.
  • measured The synaesthete's advantage on the digit grid: 41.4% against 31.5% correct (Ward et al. 2010). The same authors concluded it is not pre-attentive pop-out, and one study failed to replicate it at all. A head start, not magic.
  • measured Expertise suppresses the irrelevant more strongly than it enhances the relevant (r = −0.43 against r = +0.27, Gegenfurtner et al. 2011). Those two numbers came from a secondary source; the paper is paywalled and they are unverified.
  • modelled Everything the four readers do. Each is a mandate — an objective, a time horizon, a loss function — applied to this note by a language model, then checked against the constraints above. No expert's gaze was recorded.
  • measured These readings are reproducible. Each reader scored the note five times, in five separate sessions that could not see one another. Agreement between those runs, as Krippendorff's alpha: 0.97 credit analyst, 0.94 retail investor, 0.94 risk officer, 0.88 equity PM — all above the 0.80 convention. That measures consistency, not correctness: a reader could be reproducibly wrong and score just as well.
  • modelled The first attempt failed. Told in prose that attention was finite, the model marked almost every clause important — the equity PM came out flatter than the untrained reader, inverting the finding the page is built on. It took an arithmetic quota (at most 8 of 30 clauses strongly salient, at least 12 actively ignored) to produce expert-shaped attention. That constraint was added after seeing the failure, which is worth knowing.
  • modelled The note itself is constructed, not real, and deliberately dense with clauses that invert between mandates. That makes the effect legible, and it also makes it larger than it would be on an arbitrary document.
  • measured How much larger: about a third. The same four readers were run over a second note, held back from every tuning decision — different sector, different document, 20 clauses instead of 30. Every check passed first time, which means the method is not tuned to this one page. But on that note the readers shared 50% of what they dwelt on, against 37.5% here, and one clause divided them rather than five. The differences on this page are real and they reproduce. They are also the best case, not the typical one.

The largest weakness

No published eye-tracking study compares a credit analyst, an equity PM, a risk officer and a retail investor reading the same document. It appears not to exist. The differences between these four readers are argued from their mandates, not measured. The expert-versus-novice structure is borrowed from a meta-analysis of medicine, sport and aviation, and applying it to financial prose is an assumption no study licenses.

What is missing

No human has validated any of it. The tooling to compare a real practitioner's markup against the reader claiming to represent them exists, and has never been run against a real practitioner. Three people and one afternoon would close it, and a mismatch would be the most informative result this project could produce. Until then, every professional reading here is a language model's idea of a profession.
