Results summary · perceptual expertise in financial text

Behind someone else's eyes

Four professional mandates were applied to the same page of financial text, five independent times each, and checked against constraints taken from the eye-tracking literature. This is what the numbers are, how they were produced, and how much weight they will bear.

2 documents · 30 and 20 clauses 4 reader mandates 60 independent scoring runs 198 tests

One · The result

Experts do not disagree about the news. They disagree about where to look.

The project was built to show a credit analyst and an equity manager reading the same sentence in opposite directions. Scored independently, that conflict did not appear — the three professionals agreed with each other on essentially every judgement. What separated them was attention. And the one place genuine disagreement did appear was not between professionals at all.

Two · Method

How the numbers were made

Each reader is defined as a mandate, not a personality: an objective, a time horizon, a loss function, and the things it reads for. This follows Haider & Frensch's finding that how much a reader discards depends on the task they believe they are doing. A credit analyst is “paid a coupon and can lose the principal”; nothing is said about temperament.

Step 01Turn the literature into tests

Published effect sizes become constraints the output must satisfy: an expert must attend more than a novice to task-relevant clauses, less to irrelevant ones, and its attention must be more concentrated. These run as an automated check, not as a guideline.

Step 02Score each reader five times, blind

Every reader scores every clause on four attributes — how strongly it pulls attention, whether it is good or bad news to them, which clauses they read as one idea, and how much alarm it raises. Each of the five runs happens in a separate session that cannot see the other four. Without that independence, no agreement statistic means anything.

Step 03Aggregate by median

The five runs combine by median rather than mean, so one wild sample cannot drag a clause. Grouping labels combine by mode instead, since they are labels and averaging them would be meaningless.

Step 04Check, and be willing to fail

The aggregate is tested against step one. A failing run is reported, not quietly fixed — the whole point of building the check first was to be able to fail it.

Step 05Repeat on a document never seen before

Anything tuned during steps one to four was tuned on one document. A second note, held back from every decision, tests whether the method transfers or merely fits.

Three · Interpretation

How to read each number

Four statistics carry the results. All are standard, computed exactly, and unit-tested against hand-worked values. The arithmetic is not where the uncertainty lives.

TOP-8 SHARED  — the headline

Of the eight clauses each reader spends most attention on, how many are the same. It answers the question a heat-map overlay answers, and needs no explanation to a non-specialist.

1 / 8 — a credit analyst and an untrained reader dwell on almost entirely different sentences.
6 / 8 — a credit analyst and a risk officer are reading nearly the same document.

CONCENTRATION  — 0 to 1

How focused a reader's attention is. Zero means spread perfectly evenly; higher means more of the attention lands on fewer clauses. The literature predicts experts score higher than novices, and here they always do.

The useful reading is the ratio, not the absolute value: a credit analyst at 0.062 against an untrained reader at 0.023 is roughly 2.7× more concentrated on the same page.

VALENCE CONFLICT

A clause where two readers both feel strongly and disagree in sign — one reads it as good news, the other as bad. Weak disagreements are excluded deliberately; noise is not conflict.

Five in the hero note, every one of them professional versus lay. Zero among the three professionals.

KRIPPENDORFF'S ALPHA  — reproducibility

How closely the five blind runs agree. Above 0.80 by convention supports firm conclusions. It measures consistency, not correctness — a reader could be reproducibly wrong and score just as well.

One trap worth knowing: Alpha divides by the spread of scores across clauses, so a reader attending near-uniformly — correct behaviour for a novice — can score near zero while agreeing perfectly. The analysis reads alpha alongside concentration to tell “diffuse” apart from “erratic”.

Five · Validation

The held-out document, and what it cost the headline

One constraint in the method was tightened after watching a run fail, which leaves an obvious objection: the readers may look expert because they were told what expert looks like, on the one document the telling was tuned to. So the whole pipeline was run again, untouched, on a second note held back from every decision — different sector, different document type, 20 clauses rather than 30, so the constraint rescaled itself without being edited.

It passed, first time

Every literature check, every reader, alpha between 0.826 and 0.961. The method is not fitted to one page. The suppression asymmetry — reported but not gated — even came out on the predicted side here, which it had not on the hero note, with nothing changed to achieve it.

The hero note was written to divide these readers. The held-out note was not. The effect survives — and it is about a third smaller.

This is the number a sceptic should ask for, so it belongs in the summary rather than a footnote. The dramatic 0/8 is a property of the constructed document, not of the four mandates. On ordinary prose the readers still separate, reliably and in the direction the literature predicts, but less theatrically.

Six · Corrections

Three things that were wrong, and what fixing them showed

These are errors in the project's own design, found only by running it. They are here because each one is more useful than the result it was blocking.

Prose does not constrain a model. Arithmetic does.

Told in words that “your attention is a finite budget”, the model marked almost every clause important. The equity manager came out at concentration 0.017 — essentially uniform, and flatter than the untrained reader, inverting the finding the project rests on.

Replacing the sentence with a countable rule — at most 8 of 30 clauses may score high, at least 12 must be actively ignored — fixed it immediately, and the runs reported hitting the quota exactly. A model's default is to be comprehensive, and comprehensiveness is the precise opposite of expertise. This is the most transferable thing here.

The novice needed a different rule, or the contrast vanished

Applying the expert quota to the untrained reader would have made the novice as focused as the expert and erased the very thing being measured. The lay reader gets a deliberately looser bound, because the literature's claim is that experts concentrate and novices do not.

The first headline statistic said the opposite of the truth

The original measure scored every pair of readers at 83–94% similar — arithmetically correct, and rhetorically fatal. It reads as these people broadly agree. The statistic simply compresses everything into a narrow band on a long document. Replacing it with top-8 overlap separated the readers properly and made the result legible.

The failed run is kept in the repository alongside the successful ones, so the before-and-after is auditable rather than asserted.

Seven · Limits

What this does not show

  1. No human has validated any of it. The tooling to compare a real practitioner's markup against the reader claiming to represent them exists and has never been run against a real practitioner. Three people and one afternoon would close it, and a mismatch would be the most informative result this project could produce. This is the single largest outstanding gap.
  2. No published study measures these roles. Nothing compares a credit analyst, an equity manager, a risk officer and a retail investor reading the same document; it appears not to exist. The differences here are argued from mandates, not measured from people.
  3. The effect sizes come from other fields — medicine, sport, aviation, music — and are assumed to transfer to financial prose. No study licenses that.
  4. Two calibration constants are unverified, taken from secondary sources because the source paper is paywalled.
  5. A language model is not an expert. Sampling five times bounds the variance. Nothing here bounds the bias.

Everything above is modelled unless stated otherwise. Two things on the companion page are genuinely measured on people: the bouba/kiki result (72% across 917 participants and 25 languages, not the ~95% usually quoted) and the synaesthete's advantage on a digit-search task (41.4% against 31.5% — real, but not the instant pop-out it is usually described as).

Reproducing it

Every sample from all three scoring runs is committed, including the failed one. The visualization holds no scores of its own — it renders whatever the pipeline emitted, so re-scoring and rebuilding is the entire update path. 

cd pipeline && uv sync && uv run pytest        # 198 tests, no network
uv run python -m cmp.from_samples scored/subagent-run2    meridian-q4
uv run python -m cmp.from_samples scored/subagent-heldout aldercroft-h1
python3 ../viz/build.py
